# Hasluck 1904 girth fastening: primary-source routing assessment

Read-only assessment by `period_1904`, 2026-10-19. This reviews the cached primary text and original engravings; no model, evaluator, geometry generation or browser was run.

The text supports an asymmetric fastening assembly: a **strap on the near side and a girth on the off side**, each stitched to the base of its saddle flap. It describes **one buckle carried on a lay at the girth end**, plus two loops. It does not establish a pair of symmetric buckles and billets at both upper girth ends. A model following this particular recipe should declare its near-side strap, off-side girth and the single adjustable closure as distinct source roles.

The source is Paul N. Hasluck, *Harness Making*, original 1904 edition, printed p. 137; retained extracted lines 6014–6037. The Project Gutenberg issue date, 2023-02-28, is the electronic publication date. The cached HTML comes from `https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GITenberg/Harness-making_70169/master/70169-h/70169-h.htm`. The original 1904 work is public domain in the USA by age; this assessment does not infer an institutional grant or derive model textures from the engravings.

> “A strap and a girth are needed to fasten to the bottom of the flaps.”
>
> “Make a buckle hole in the lay so that the edge of the buckle will be level with the end of the girth, as the chape is not to be turned down, but is stitched on the flat.”
>
> “Tack the lay on the girth with the buckle level to the end, and narrow the girth to the width of the lay at the point; then place two loops beyond the buckle and stitch the lay.”
>
> “…the strap on the near side and the girth on the off side at the base of the flaps, the stitches running across and in a half circle from corner to corner.”

In horse-handling usage, near side is the horse's left and off side its right. The retained original fixture generator explicitly declares `left: '+X'` and `up: '+Y'`. Thus **near = reconstructed `l` / +X** and **off = reconstructed `r` / −X**, using the horse's own sides. This coordinate mapping is an explicit model convention; the primary book does not contain an XYZ frame. Camera left/right must not reverse the assignment.

I personally re-viewed the retained original `hasluck1904-131-4.jpg`. Its captions are **Fig. 182—Van Saddle; Fig. 183—Van Saddle Flap; Fig. 184—Van Saddle Panel**, printed p. 131. These distinguish the saddle, flap and panel and show their outline/stitch vocabulary. They do not show a complete fitted under-barrel girth closure or resolve every layer/loop direction in the p. 137 instructions. Adjacent text distinguishes panel fastening for gig/cab saddles from a van saddle; the illustrated van construction should not be silently treated as an identified Chicago buggy object.

I also re-viewed `hasluck1904-147-3.jpg`, **Fig. 190—Back-band**. It is a separate component and is useful only as a buckle/keeper/strap-hole comparator. Its visible loops, length and hardware layout do not override the girth-specific two-loop and one-buckle instructions. It is not an image of the completed proposed upper attachment.

The current qualified U-girth is a separate reconstructed visual shell. Its ends stop at Y = 1.21 m and remain about 38.3–44.3 mm from symmetric existing hardware. Those gaps are not seams. A source-led next proposal should first distinguish the off-side girth-to-flap stitched end, the near-side strap-to-flap stitched end, and the adjustable girth/strap closure. Fitting either endpoint to an existing nickel frame merely because that geometry is nearby would not follow the passage. Exact lengths around our horse, buckle location, overlap, underside route, seam/loop geometry, finish, thickness and contact clearances remain explicit reconstruction choices until separately justified and checked.

The passage is period vocabulary and a manufacturing recipe, not a photographic Chicago reference, a measured object survey, a veterinary fit specification or proof of a completed load-bearing hitch. It does not attest the unseen Moore 1891 plate, and provides no basis for backdating the proposed arrangement into 1812 or 1835. Another documented harness type could use a different arrangement, but would need its own source instead of being attributed to this passage.
