PHASE AND CAPTION VERIFICATION
Andreas p.358 engraving caption exactly: "CHAMBER OF COMMERCE."
The caption itself supplies no date. Its phase is established by placement in the narrative headed "THE YEAR 1865–66" on p.357, immediately above p.358's paragraph stating that this building was completed August 28, 1865, and by the same distinctive facade seen in the museum's dated 1866–67 lithograph. Both show five tall round-headed north windows arranged two/one/two, a triple-arched main entrance, projecting quoins and an oculus-bearing mansard front. This identifies the original 1865 building, not the 1872 replacement, 1885 Jackson/LaSalle Board of Trade building, or 1890 third Chamber of Commerce. The engraving's own creation date and source image remain unidentified; do not claim that it was made in 1865 or independently photographed.

Read chronology as distinct events:
- September 11, 1864: cornerstone laid (Andreas p.357).
- August 13, 1865: Board of Trade first occupied its rooms, as printed (p.357). This is not the completion date; independent confirmation was not sought in this bounded pass.
- August 28, 1865: building completed (p.358).
- August 30, 1865: three-day inauguration jubilee began (p.357); museum calls this dedication.
- 1866–67: museum's date for the Kurz/Jevne & Almini lithograph.
- October 1871: original destroyed, per existing dataset and museum chronology. The exact destruction time was not independently established here.
- October 9, 1872: Board of Trade returned to its replacement, per museum interpretation. Explicitly exclude this later building and the museum's circa-1872 roof-construction slide.

MATERIALS, ACCESS AND FORM
Andreas states cut stone, called "Athens marble", on north/east/west, and brick on the south facing Calhoun Place. Preserve that historic material term; it does not independently establish modern geological classification or exact surface colour. The main Washington entrance opens onto the central corridor, with first-story business offices on both sides. A high, well-lit basement also contains offices and has side street entrances. LaSalle and Exchange Place side entrances lead to the main corridor and second-story stairs; exact facade positions still need image/map reconciliation.
Above first story, large projecting quoins divide the north facade into three parts: one central circular-topped triple window, with two smaller similarly ornamented windows on each flank. The exchange hall is on the second story, with ten windows on either side and five at the north. The south gallery and office strip are described. The account also mentions steam heating, evening ceiling reflectors and frescoes, which do not establish lit interiors or occupied exchange activity at 16:00 on Sunday, October 8.

WHAT THE IMAGES SHOW
The museum lithograph was directly inspected. It shows the north/front and west/LaSalle side from an inferred northwest viewpoint. The west side recedes to image right and is partly obscured by a large foreground tree. Visible features include the triple-arched north portal, two flanking ground-floor window groups, the five very tall upper arch heads, rusticated quoins and horizontal masonry joints, strongly projecting decorated cornice, steep mansard face with four small round openings flanking a raised central circular feature, an ornamental crest and flagstaff. Signboards include the Chamber of Commerce and Solomon Sturges Sons; these lettering/tenancy observations are dated to the pictured scene, not automatically to October 1871. Exact lettering beyond clearly readable words needs higher-resolution confirmation.
The Andreas p.358 engraving shows a clearer, unobscured west elevation, north portal and basement zone, including railings and a side doorway. It is useful for camera fitting and roof/window rhythm. It remains a drawn view; its viewpoint, lens equivalent and possible drafting distortions must be solved, not assumed. Its relationship to the earlier lithograph is unestablished, so do not count them as two independent measured surveys. Neither image shows the east and south elevations completely. Trees, horses, wagons, people, flags and pavement illustrate the pictured streetscape, not a witnessed October 8 event or an exact traffic count.

UNRESOLVED PRINTED HEIGHT CONTRADICTION
The contradictory text is visibly present in the printed page; it is not just an OCR mistake. P.358 right column reads:
"The whole building was surmounted by a Mansard roof, the extreme height of which was twenty-three feet above the cornice and ninety-nine feet from the basement floor. The cornice was ninety-nine feet from the basement floor."
These statements cannot all describe the same vertical geometry. Do not choose roof=99 ft, cornice=99 ft, or an inferred 76-ft cornice merely because one looks plausible. Preserve both claims and seek a contemporary description, section/elevation, or independent measurable picture before selecting an absolute height. Street/sidewalk versus basement datum also remains unresolved. Camera fitting should retain this uncertainty explicitly.
